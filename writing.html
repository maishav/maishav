Analytical Reports & Field Work
2025 — 2026
Innovision Consulting Private Limited, Dhaka
Turning Waste into Clean Energy: A Circular Economy Case Study

Founded in the mid-2010s, this Bangladeshi enterprise emerged in response to a largely invisible but serious problem in Bangladesh's food system: the unsafe reuse and disposal of used cooking oil. Repeatedly reused oil can become carcinogenic, while improper disposal contaminates water bodies and drains, posing risks to public health and the environment. The company developed a model to systematically collect used cooking oil from restaurants and convert it into biodiesel, a renewable alternative to fossil fuel. What began as an early-stage idea gradually evolved into a scalable circular economy solution that addresses waste management, clean energy production, and climate mitigation simultaneously. The company engaged with an international youth entrepreneurship innovation platform during its early growth phase, when the business was still exploring viable pathways to scale. Through this platform's ecosystem linkages and international exposure — including engagements across multiple South and Southeast Asian markets — the founders gained critical insights into global renewable fuel markets. Prior to this exposure, exporting was not part of the company's strategic vision. Within a few years of formal registration as a limited company, the enterprise began scaling operations nationally. Today, it operates a nationwide network of approximately 1,900 restaurant collection points across Bangladesh. On average, it exports around 100 tons of processed oil per month to international markets across Asia and Europe. Its products are integrated into global renewable fuel supply chains, including feedstock for Sustainable Aviation Fuel (SAF), and the company undergoes annual international sustainability audits. According to the founder, biodiesel produced from recycled cooking oil can reduce carbon dioxide emissions by approximately 80 percent compared to mineral diesel. This makes the model particularly relevant for hard-to-abate sectors such as aviation, where rapid infrastructure replacement is not feasible. Beyond environmental benefits, the model creates economic value across the supply chain. What was once a waste product has become an income source for restaurants and low-income restaurant workers. The enterprise indirectly supports around 1,900 individuals within the restaurant ecosystem through waste-to-income linkages. From a business perspective, the enterprise has grown significantly — moving from a modest monthly revenue base during its early platform engagement to a substantial annual export value today. While the innovation platform did not provide direct funding, it played a catalytic role by expanding the company's market outlook, credibility, and global connectivity. This case illustrates how locally rooted innovation, when supported by credible networks and exposure, can scale into a globally relevant climate solution.

Saint Martin's: Protecting Nature Through Smart Policy

Bangladesh's government recently launched a 3-year conservation initiative for Saint Martin's Island, the country's only coral island, introducing a tourist conservation fee alongside a zoning plan, plastic bans, and livelihood support for local families. This piece unpacks the policy as a practical case of environmental economics in action — using pricing to internalize the ecological cost of tourism rather than restricting access outright. It reflects on why "putting a value on nature" is often the more sustainable path to protecting it than an outright ban.
