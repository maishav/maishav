Reading

Books that shaped how I think.

I read across development economics, environmental policy, and narrative non-fiction. These are not summaries — they are the questions each book left me with.

"The best economics writing clarifies what was always there but unseen."

Genres I follow

Development Economics Environmental Policy Narrative Non-fiction Gender & Society Data & Science Historical Economics

Poor
Economics

Poor Economics: A Radical Rethinking of the Way to Fight Global Poverty

Abhijit V. Banerjee & Esther Duflo · 2011
★★★★★
Development Economics Field Experiments

What stays with me is not the policy prescriptions but the method — the insistence that you cannot design interventions for the poor without first sitting with them and watching what they actually do. Banerjee and Duflo dismantled my assumption that poverty is primarily a resource problem. It is also, deeply, an information and incentive problem. I return to this book every time I am designing a field instrument.

→ Essential reading before any field research in development contexts.

Why
Nations
Fail

Why Nations Fail: The Origins of Power, Prosperity, and Poverty

Daron Acemoglu & James A. Robinson · 2012
★★★★★
Political Economy Institutions Historical Analysis

The central argument — that inclusive institutions, not geography or culture, determine long-run prosperity — is compelling but demands scrutiny. As someone researching coastal Bangladesh, I found myself asking: what does "inclusion" look like when the institutional failures are layered atop climate vulnerability? The framework holds, but it needs more texture when applied to South Asian contexts where informality is not dysfunction but adaptation.

→ Argues persuasively, though the granularity needed for field work requires supplementary reading.

The Sixth
Extinction

The Sixth Extinction: An Unnatural History

Elizabeth Kolbert · 2014
★★★★★
Environmental Economics Narrative Non-fiction Climate

Kolbert does what good economic writing rarely manages: she makes the stakes visceral. Chapter by chapter, she reports from the front lines of extinction events that are already underway — and the cumulative effect is not despair but a kind of sharpened responsibility. For someone studying environmental behaviour in coastal communities, this book was a reminder that the "environment" in environmental economics is not an abstraction. It is a system already in crisis.

→ Should be required reading alongside any environmental economics syllabus.

Fact-
fulness

Factfulness: Ten Reasons We're Wrong About the World

Hans Rosling · 2018
★★★★★
Data & Statistics Development

Rosling's optimism is evidence-based, which makes it genuinely persuasive rather than merely reassuring. The ten instincts he identifies — gap, negativity, straight line — are as useful as any methodological checklist. I now think about the "fear instinct" every time I write a research brief: am I presenting risk accurately, or am I amplifying it because alarming findings attract attention? The book changed how I communicate data to non-specialist audiences.

→ Read this before writing your next research summary for policymakers.
